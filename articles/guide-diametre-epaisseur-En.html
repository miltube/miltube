How to choose the inside diameter and wall thickness of a paper core

The wrong diameter or wall thickness can stop a production line, deform a roll, or get a shipment rejected. Here's how to size both before you order.

Inside diameter: set by your machine, not by your product

The core's inside diameter (ID) must match your machine's winding shaft or arbor, with a very small clearance, typically under 1 mm. An ID that's too loose makes the roll vibrate on the shaft. An ID that's too tight makes the core hard to fit, and can damage it.

The most common industrial diameters follow standards close to whole inches:

  • 50 and 60 mm — small format, for labels, adhesive tapes, thin stretch film.
  • 76 mm (3″) — the most widespread standard, for industrial packaging and general film winding.
  • 100 mm — wide-format printing, heavier general winding.
  • 152 mm (6″) and 279 mm (11″) — agricultural films, heavy textile, mill-sized paper rolls, metal coils.

If you don't know your shaft's exact diameter, measure it directly, or send us a core you already use — we'll match it exactly.

Wall thickness: driven by winding tension, not just roll weight

This is the most common mistake: assuming wall thickness only needs to support the finished roll's weight. In reality, the deciding factor is the radial tension applied during winding, especially for stretch film, which grips the core tightly.

  • 3 mm — light packaging, low-tension products, short storage.
  • 5 to 9 mm — standard use, most film and paper applications.
  • up to 17 mm — heavy industrial applications: metal coils, stacked vertical storage, long-distance transport.

A wall that's too thin under a high-tension film shows up as a core that ovalizes or crushes within the first few winding turns. An oversized wall, on the other hand, adds unnecessary weight and cost with no real benefit.

Three questions to ask before ordering

  • What is the exact diameter of my winding shaft? Measure it, don't estimate it.
  • How much tension does my material put on the core? Stretch film pulls far harder than light paper.
  • How is the roll stored and transported? Prolonged vertical stacking needs more axial strength than horizontal storage.

Test before committing

Before any volume order, good practice is to test a core on your actual line: check the fit on the shaft, watch how it behaves under tension while winding, and check straightness after a few days in storage.
